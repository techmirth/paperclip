# A, B, and C Players: A Placement Guide for Hiring Managers

Every team is a mix of people operating at different levels relative to the role
they're in. The "A / B / C player" framework is a fast, shared vocabulary for
talking about that mix — not to label people permanently, but to make better
decisions about **where each person belongs**.

A few principles before the definitions:

- **The tier is relative to a role, not a person's worth.** Someone can be a C
  player in one seat and an A player in another. Placement is the lever, not
  judgment.
- **The bar is "top talent for this role at this pay range."** An A player isn't
  "good," they're the person you'd fight to keep and feel lucky to have for what
  the seat costs.
- **Tiers move.** The point of naming them is to act: stretch the A's, develop
  the B's, and resolve the C's quickly and humanely.

---

## A Players — Raise the ceiling

**Definition.** The top performers for the role and pay range — roughly the
people you'd re-hire instantly and would be alarmed to lose. They deliver
outcomes, not just effort, and they raise the performance of everyone around
them.

**How to recognize one**

- Owns outcomes end to end; you delegate a goal, not a checklist.
- Makes the people around them better — through standards, mentoring, or sheer
  clarity.
- Needs context and direction, not supervision. Surfaces problems early with a
  proposed fix attached.
- Track record of results that survived contact with reality, across more than
  one situation.

**Where to place them**

- On the **highest-leverage, most ambiguous problems** — the ones where the path
  isn't defined yet. A players convert ambiguity into direction.
- In **roles with headroom.** Their biggest risk is boredom; a seat they've
  already outgrown will lose them.
- Near other A players. They calibrate to the best person in the room and
  decay in isolation.

**How to keep them**

- Give them scope, autonomy, and honest feedback (they crave it).
- Pay at the top of the band before they ask. A players know their market value.
- Promote on trajectory, not tenure.

**Hiring signal to interview for:** evidence of raising the bar — initiatives
they started, people they grew, problems they owned that no one assigned them.

## B Players — The backbone

**Definition.** Solid, dependable contributors who do the job well and
consistently. Most of any healthy organization is B players, and that's a
feature, not a problem — a company of all A players is often unstable.

**How to recognize one**

- Reliable. The work gets done, on time, at a known quality bar.
- Strong in a defined scope; thrives with clear expectations and good systems.
- Steadies the team — institutional memory, low drama, dependable judgment in
  familiar territory.
- May not seek the spotlight or the stretch, and that's fine.

**Where to place them**

- In roles where **consistency and reliability matter more than reinvention** —
  operations, delivery, maintenance, customer-facing steadiness.
- As **the stable core of a team** that has one or two A players pushing the
  edges. The mix is the point.
- In seats with clear expectations and good support. B players are made or
  broken by the **system around them** more than A players are.

**How to grow them**

- Don't assume the ceiling is fixed. Many "B players" are A players in the wrong
  seat, under a weak manager, or never given a real stretch. Test that before
  concluding.
- Invest in development, clear feedback, and a path. A neglected B player drifts
  toward C; a supported one can become an A.

**Hiring signal to interview for:** consistency and follow-through — a steady
record of doing what they said they would, well, over time.

## C Players — Resolve quickly and humanely

**Definition.** People who are not meeting the bar for the role at the pay range
— for whatever reason. C is the most important tier to get right, because the
**cost of leaving a C player in a critical seat is paid by the whole team.**

**How to recognize one**

- Outcomes consistently fall short of what the role requires, after fair time and
  support.
- Creates more work than they remove — others quietly compensate, rework, or
  route around them.
- Low standards spread. A tolerated C player tells your A and B players what's
  acceptable, and your best people leave first.

**The crucial distinction — diagnose before you decide**

Most C-player situations are **placement or context problems, not people
problems.** Before concluding "C player," rule out:

- **Wrong seat.** Strong skills aimed at the wrong work. → Re-place them.
- **Unclear expectations.** They don't know what "good" looks like. → Fix the
  clarity, then re-evaluate.
- **Weak system or manager.** No support, no feedback, broken tools. → Fix the
  environment first.
- **Ramp.** New and still climbing the curve. → Give a defined, time-boxed runway.

If, after a fair and honest attempt at the above, the bar still isn't met:
**act.** The kindest thing for the person and the team is a clear, prompt
resolution — re-placement if there's a real fit elsewhere, or a respectful exit
if there isn't.

**Where to place them**

- First choice: **a seat that fits their actual strengths**, if one genuinely
  exists. Re-placement is the underused win.
- Never in a **critical or high-leverage seat** while the gap is unresolved. The
  blast radius is too large.
- Not parked indefinitely. Drift is the expensive option — it costs morale,
  standards, and your A players.

**Hiring signal to interview for:** you're trying to *avoid* a future C-player
hire. Screen for the specific outcomes the role requires and check them against
real evidence — not credentials, charisma, or general competence.

## Quick placement reference

| Tier | One-line read | Best placement | Default move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Top talent; raises the bar | Highest-leverage, most ambiguous work, with headroom | Stretch, pay, retain |
| **B** | Reliable backbone | Roles rewarding consistency; stable core of a team | Develop, support, clarify the path |
| **C** | Below the bar for the seat | A seat that fits — or out | Diagnose, re-place, or resolve promptly |

## Using this well

1. **Rate the role, not the human.** Always ask "A/B/C *for this seat*." The same
   person can change tier with a change of placement.
2. **Diagnose before you label.** Wrong seat, unclear expectations, weak manager,
   and short ramp account for most apparent C players. Fix the environment first.
3. **Act on what you find.** The framework is only useful if it leads to a
   decision — a stretch assignment, a development plan, a re-placement, or a
   respectful exit.
4. **Protect your A and B players.** They are watching how you handle C-player
   situations. Tolerating a clear, unresolvable gap is the fastest way to lose
   the people you most want to keep.
