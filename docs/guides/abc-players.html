A, B, and C Players

A placement guide for managers classifying their team. The tier describes a person in a seat — not their worth. Used well, it leads to a decision: stretch, develop, re-place, or resolve.

Three principles first
  • The tier is relative to a role, not a person. Someone can be a C player in one seat and an A player in another. Placement is the lever.
  • The bar is "top talent for this role at this pay range" — not just "good." An A player is someone you'd fight to keep for what the seat costs.
  • Tiers move. The point of naming them is to act on them.

ARaise the ceiling — top talent for the seat

Definition. The top performers for the role and pay range — the people you'd re-hire instantly and would be alarmed to lose. They deliver outcomes, not just effort, and they raise the performance of everyone around them.

How to recognize one

  • Owns outcomes end to end; you delegate a goal, not a checklist.
  • Makes the people around them better — through standards, mentoring, or clarity.
  • Needs context and direction, not supervision. Surfaces problems early with a fix attached.
  • Track record of results that survived contact with reality, across more than one situation.

Where to place them

  • On the highest-leverage, most ambiguous problems — where the path isn't defined yet.
  • In roles with headroom. Their biggest risk is boredom; an outgrown seat loses them.
  • Near other A players. They calibrate to the best person in the room and decay in isolation.

How to keep them

  • Give scope, autonomy, and honest feedback (they crave it).
  • Pay at the top of the band before they ask. Promote on trajectory, not tenure.

Interview for: evidence of raising the bar — initiatives they started, people they grew, problems they owned that no one assigned them.

BThe backbone — reliable, consistent

Definition. Solid, dependable contributors who do the job well and consistently. Most of any healthy organization is B players — and that's a feature. A company of all A players is often unstable.

How to recognize one

  • Reliable. The work gets done, on time, at a known quality bar.
  • Strong in a defined scope; thrives with clear expectations and good systems.
  • Steadies the team — institutional memory, low drama, dependable judgment in familiar territory.
  • May not seek the spotlight or the stretch — and that's fine.

Where to place them

  • In roles where consistency matters more than reinvention — operations, delivery, maintenance, customer-facing steadiness.
  • As the stable core of a team that has one or two A players pushing the edges. The mix is the point.
  • In seats with clear expectations and support. B players are made or broken by the system around them more than A players are.

How to grow them

  • Don't assume the ceiling is fixed. Many "B players" are A players in the wrong seat, under a weak manager, or never given a real stretch. Test that before concluding.
  • A neglected B player drifts toward C; a supported one can become an A.

Interview for: consistency and follow-through — a steady record of doing what they said they would, well, over time.

CResolve quickly and humanely — below the bar for the seat

Definition. People not meeting the bar for the role at the pay range — for whatever reason. C is the most important tier to get right, because the cost of leaving a C player in a critical seat is paid by the whole team.

How to recognize one

  • Outcomes consistently fall short of what the role requires, after fair time and support.
  • Creates more work than they remove — others quietly compensate, rework, or route around them.
  • Low standards spread. A tolerated C player tells your A and B players what's acceptable, and your best people leave first.
Diagnose before you decide. Most C-player situations are placement or context problems, not people problems. Before concluding "C player," rule out:
  • Wrong seat. Strong skills aimed at the wrong work. → Re-place them.
  • Unclear expectations. They don't know what "good" looks like. → Fix the clarity, then re-evaluate.
  • Weak system or manager. No support, no feedback, broken tools. → Fix the environment first.
  • Ramp. New and still climbing the curve. → Give a defined, time-boxed runway.
If, after a fair attempt at the above, the bar still isn't met: act. The kindest thing for the person and the team is a prompt, clear resolution — re-placement if there's a real fit elsewhere, or a respectful exit if there isn't.

Where to place them

  • First choice: a seat that fits their actual strengths, if one genuinely exists. Re-placement is the underused win.
  • Never in a critical or high-leverage seat while the gap is unresolved. The blast radius is too large.
  • Not parked indefinitely. Drift costs morale, standards, and your A players.

Interview for: you're trying to avoid a future C-player hire. Screen for the specific outcomes the role requires and check them against real evidence — not credentials, charisma, or general competence.

Using this well

  • Rate the role, not the human. Always ask "A/B/C for this seat." The same person can change tier with a change of placement.
  • Diagnose before you label. Wrong seat, unclear expectations, weak manager, and short ramp account for most apparent C players. Fix the environment first.
  • Act on what you find. The framework is only useful if it leads to a decision — a stretch, a development plan, a re-placement, or a respectful exit.
  • Protect your A and B players. They're watching how you handle C-player situations. Tolerating a clear, unresolvable gap is the fastest way to lose the people you most want to keep.
